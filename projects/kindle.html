Kindle App Redesign: browse books by tag, not just genre

Redesigning the Kindle app's filtering system to include tags, bringing the language readers already use into the app, right where they buy.

Role
Solo Product Designer
Responsibilities
End-to-end design process
Timeline
April 2026
Tools
Figma, FigJam, Claude
01

Overview

How readers find their next book.

Kindle app users consistently abandon the platform's discovery tools and migrate to Reddit, TikTok and third-party apps to complete the task Amazon should own: finding a book to read next.

This recurring pattern shows the platform's filtering system is failing, especially on mobile. Recommendations are often books users have already read or that haven't come out yet.

Most importantly, it offers no way to search by trope, which has become a prominent way for modern readers to discover books.

02

Defining the problem

Understanding where the current experience fails.

I performed a design audit of the Kindle app and the Amazon Kindle web storefront to identify existing issues and inconsistencies. I found hierarchy issues that completely hide some features, and vast inconsistencies between the app and web platform.

01

Filtering requires too many steps

The app requires three taps and a scroll to reach subgenres; the web storefront reaches the same point in two clicks. Subgenres are hidden below the fold, and users who don't scroll don't know they exist.

02

Recommendations aren't trustworthy

"Recommended for You" surfaces unreleased titles with no way to filter them out. Research found users describing up to 80% of suggestions as books they already own. Neither platform lets you filter out "previously downloaded," a pain point for Kindle Unlimited members.

03

The app doesn't speak the reader's language

Readers now search with trope and mood vocabulary ("enemies to lovers," "slow burn," "dark academia") driven by BookTok and communities like r/suggestmeabook. Users leaving the app entirely during discovery is a key issue for stakeholders.

03

The discovery journey

Expanding the design audit to competitors.

With a baseline in place, I used Claude to compare the Kindle app to other reading and discovery platforms.

Competitive takeaway

The Kindle app is severely behind on discovery features and isn't capitalizing on the demand for trope-based filtering.

04

Design exploration

Which layout and hierarchy best showcase the design features?

Based on user and market research, I identified the features that would have the greatest impact:

01

Subgenres and tropes visible on load

A horizontal pill row at the top of the category page, similar to the web storefront's sticky bar, resolves the fold-line problem immediately.

02

A trope/mood tag system on book pages

Tags applied by publishers and authors make trope-based browsing native to the app.

03

A multi-select, persistent filter system

Users need to combine criteria. Drawing on StoryGraph and Amazon's web experience, a filter drawer or sticky filter bar with multi-select addresses the single largest structural failure in the app.

04

Filter out already-read and unreleased books by default

Purchased and pre-order-only titles are excluded from recommendation feeds by default, with an opt-in toggle.

05

Clickable tags with circular discovery

Tags on a book page link to more books with the same trope.

Design variations

Using my initial wireframe sketches and user research, I prompted Claude to create five mid-fidelity design directions. Claude accelerates iteration, but human auditing ensures the output holds up to design-thinking principles.

For example, Claude initially proposed making tags the entry point, but that's too drastic a change for too little reward. Many users might find tags confusing or simply not want to use them.

05

Structured AI walkthrough

Which version, or combination, would produce the best user experience?

As a solo designer on a short turnaround, I ran five independent evaluation passes with Claude, each walking through a variation as a Kindle user trying to buy a new book. I asked it to:

  • Identify which directions, or aspects of each, are most valuable based on usability risk and potential impact.
  • Consider recurring, realistic user behavior, patterns and areas of confusion across participants.
  • Evaluate business impact for Amazon in addition to user experience.

Tropes discovered passively

Navigated genre, saw the trope row on load, and used it without ever opening the drawer.

Circular discovery works unprompted

Tapped a tag on the book detail page without guidance and bought a different book found through it.

Result count ambiguity

After closing the filter drawer, was unsure whether the count reflected active filters or the whole category.

Chevron affordance missed

Three persona simulations tapped the genre name instead of the chevron in L5, never finding the drill-down level.

Multi-select unclear

Tried to tap a second trope pill and wasn't sure it would behave as multi-select.

Trope vocabulary gap

Stalled on a panel labelled "Tropes"; the word doesn't map to how non-fiction readers think.

Walkthrough review

During the evaluation, I found a flaw in the L5 prototype's logic: Claude assumed users would need to tap the chevron icon specifically to open the drill-down menu. On mobile, a larger tap surface is standard practice, so I clarified that the tappable area should extend beyond the icon, which increases the success rate for that interaction.

Key design insight

"Tags," not "tropes"

Trope vocabulary doesn't translate to non-fiction, and readers who aren't active on social media may not know the terms at all. "Tags" is a more neutral term that applies to every genre and is understood by most users.

06

Final design

How the walkthrough shaped the design.

The walkthrough surfaced two additional features. Result counts on tag pills, showing how many books match before you tap, was the highest-rated individual feature, removing uncertainty about whether a niche filter returns useful results. And a grid/list toggle let users see tags in list view without opening each book.

Entry point

Genre / category first

Genre was the most intuitive entry point, so tags live inside it rather than replacing it.

Scannability

Tags on list view

Users toggle between the default grid and a list view that shows each book's tags.

Filter menu

Closer to the web experience

Filter by release date, genre, subgenre and tag, with multi-select and result counts. Previously downloaded and purchased books are filtered out by default.

Circular discovery

Tags open cross-genre results

Tapping a tag opens cross-genre results, while "See all" on a shelf stays scoped to the current genre.

07

Reflection

An AI-assisted workflow

AI is a useful tool for quick iteration and synthesizing research, but strong design-thinking fundamentals are still essential to prompt it well and audit what it produces. The L5 correction shows this: what looked like a user failing to find a feature was actually the AI defaulting to a smaller tap target than mobile conventions call for, a gap I caught in my own review, not in the walkthrough itself.

Proposed user testing

With more time and user access, I'd validate these findings with five real readers in a moderated, think-aloud usability study: no navigation guidance, eight minutes per direction, with direction order randomized per participant and a ten-minute break between sessions.
